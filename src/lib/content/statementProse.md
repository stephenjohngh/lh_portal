# HRB Occupation — Safety Obligations, Inspections and Assurance Controls

**The full register of statutory duties, standards-based inspections, contractual requirements and
internal assurance controls that we have decided apply to this building during occupation.**

> **Status — the register is complete; building-specific determinations remain open.**
> Every duty and control we believe applies to this building is in §6, and no gap in the
> statutory architecture is known to be outstanding. What is **not** closed is a set of facts
> about the building itself, which only a survey or a competent technical authority can settle:
> the evacuation strategy · fire alarm coverage and cause-and-effect · corridor and lobby smoke
> ventilation · the suppression design basis · the dry riser test regime · compartmentation
> drawings and survey · penetration control. Each is an action in §8 with its consequence stated,
> and **A1 and A2 gate the evacuation plan**, which cannot be signed off while the strategy and
> the alarm arrangement are unsettled.
>
> ⚠ **These are deliberately NOT resolved inside the register.** A catalogue that guessed at them
> would read as settled, and the guess would be indistinguishable afterwards from a determination.

> **On the title.** This is deliberately not called a list of "periodic obligations". A quarter of
> the entries are our own controls rather than obligations, and several are event-triggered rather
> than periodic — calling the whole set "obligations" invites someone to read our own decisions as
> law, which is the exact confusion this document is built to prevent.

## 1. What this is, and what we are asking

This is the full list of **duties and controls we believe apply to this building**: what each is,
what makes it fall due, how often, the reference it rests on, who is responsible, and what evidence
we keep.

**What would help most, in order:**

1. **Anything missing.** A duty nobody has thought of is the failure this list exists to prevent,
   and the one thing we cannot find by checking our own work. Review has repeatedly found such
   things, and each time it was the most valuable part of the review — so if only one question gets
   attention, make it this one.
2. **Anything that does not apply to a building like this**, so we can record a reasoned decision
   rather than carry a requirement we will never meet.
3. **Intervals that are wrong.** Each row says whether its frequency is stated in the reference or is
   established practice. The second kind is where we are most likely to be out, and we have already
   had to correct several rows that called a convention a legal requirement.
4. **References that are wrong.** Every one has been checked against legislation.gov.uk, and review
   has still found more.
5. **Duties we have described as cycles that are really triggers.** A duty that fires on a change, a
   request or a direction appears on no schedule, so an annual confirmation quietly becomes the
   apparent trigger and the real one is never watched for. If a row's honest answer to *"what makes
   this fall due"* is "an event, but we check it annually", we would rather be told.
6. **Anything in §2 that is wrong about the building.** Two things there are open rather than
   settled and are flagged as such: what the **residential corridors** are provided with for smoke
   ventilation, and the reason for three **stair cadences** we have deliberately not relaxed. An
   earlier contradiction about the staircases has been resolved — §2 now states the position as
   confirmed by the duty holder.

⚠ **One group has never been checked by anyone.** The nine rows in §6 *Conditional and interim
measures* were **missing from the earlier copies that went out for review**, through a fault
in how this document was assembled. No review has assessed whether those measures are right; the
comment they have attracted since is about **what they are missing** — escalation thresholds, end
conditions, residual risk — which is a different question. Every one is marked **operationally
incomplete** in §6.

Several are interim mitigations tied to open structural and fire-alarm findings. **Please treat them
as new material** rather than as already-agreed, and note that what they need is a technical reading
rather than a legal one.

### Whose register this is

Worth stating plainly, because it decides how every row below should be read.

**This is the accountable person's register**, kept for a higher-risk building in occupation. It
lists every duty and control **that bears on this building**, including duties whose statutory
dutyholder is somebody else — most importantly the **responsible person** under the Fire Safety
Order, who is not automatically the same party as the accountable person or the managing agent.

So the **"Responsible"** field on a row names **who the duty sits with**, not who holds this
document. Where that is the responsible person, our interest is in knowing the duty is discharged and
holding evidence that it was — not in performing it ourselves. Rows marked as an **interface** go
further still: the record lives in another party's system by design, and what crosses to us is that
it exists, is current, who owns it, and when it was last confirmed.

⛔ **The scope is COMMUNAL.** This register covers the **common parts, the structure, and the
building's own systems**. It does not cover inspections inside individual flats: those duties sit
with each leaseholder, or with the landlord of each individually let flat, separately and one by
one, and neither the accountable person nor the managing agent is in a position to discharge them or
to evidence that they were discharged. **So no row here concerns the interior of a dwelling, and
none is needed to say so** — the scope is the answer.

The **flat entrance door** is not an exception to that. It is the compartment boundary between the
dwelling and the common escape route, so its failure is a common-parts failure, and FSER reg 10(4)
places the duty on the **responsible person** expressly rather than on the occupier. **The line
falls at the door.**

**The scope limits what we schedule, not what we are interested in.** A hazard anywhere in the
building can be a building safety risk capable of spreading, and three rows in §6 exist to catch one
wherever it arises: the building safety risk assessment, the occurrence triage, and the statutory
right to request access to a dwelling.

⚠ **That last one is a power exercised for a purpose, and never a periodic inspection** — a
distinction worth stating because it is one step from *"management can enter a flat"* to *"management
should be inspecting flats"*. **Building Safety Act 2022 s.97** opens only to facilitate a duty under
s.83 or s.84, or to determine whether a resident duty under s.95 has been contravened; the request
must be in writing, state the purpose, explain why entry is necessary for it, propose a reasonable
time and give at least **48 hours'** notice, with the county court as the route if entry is refused.
It is keyed to suspicion, a complaint, a change or an apparent contravention — not to a date.

Checked against the instruments, **nothing imposes a periodic duty to inspect the inside of a
dwelling** on the accountable person or the managing agent: s.83 requires assessment at regular
intervals but only *"as regards the part of the building for which they are responsible"*, and
SI 2023/907 reg 4 asks for systems inspecting **the measures taken**, not the dwellings. **The one
periodic duty that reaches through a front door is the flat entrance door** — FSER reg 10(4), best
endeavours at least every 12 months — which is a common parts measure reached through a door, not an
inspection of the home behind it.

⚠ **The Building Safety Act regime does not replace the Fire Safety Order regime**, and this register
does not treat the two as one. Where a row could be read either way, ask which instrument it cites.

**The single "Responsible" field has been split, and the half that could be answered has been.**
Every row now states the **statutory dutyholder** — who bears the duty in law — separately from who
is **operationally responsible** for performing it.

⚠ **This was a misstatement, not a missing feature.** "Responsible: Site staff" stood on eight rows
whose statutory duty is the Responsible Person's, and a reader could take it as the duty having been
transferred to a cleaner. It cannot be. **The duty stays where the instrument puts it, however the
work is arranged**, and the register was implying otherwise.

**Three further fields are still outstanding, and deliberately so:** the **evidence holder**, the
**assurance owner** who confirms the duty operated, and the **escalation recipient** who decides
what happens when it fails. The difference is not effort. **Who bears a duty in law follows from the
instrument, so a catalogue can hold it; who arranges it, holds the evidence, checks it and escalates
a failure are facts about this building's arrangements** — they belong to the live obligation (§3),
and inventing them here would be fiction. They are tracked as **A5**.

## 2. The building

The building is an occupied higher-risk residential building in England within the Building Safety
Act Part 4 regime (at least 18 metres or 7 storeys, with at least 2 residential units). It is also
within the 11-metre-plus provisions of the Fire Safety (England) Regulations 2022. 8 residential
floors, 2 basement level car park with sprinkler, lift, 2 staircases.

**Also installed**, and relevant to what follows: a **lightning protection system**, a fire
detection and alarm system throughout the common parts, emergency lighting, and a dry riser.
**There is no gas supply** — the building is all-electric, and no gas duty appears in this register.

**Smoke ventilation of the staircases is about to change, and the register is built to carry that.**
Both staircases are currently ventilated **naturally, by openable windows**. A **mechanical smoke
control system (AOV) is expected but is not installed today.**

That is why §6 holds **both** provisions as separate rows — servicing for a mechanical system, and
checking for openable windows — with the mechanical one recorded as *not applicable at present*.
When the system is commissioned, the change is **two recorded decisions and no re-drafting**: the
mechanical row is reinstated, and the window row is reconsidered for each stair. Neither row is
deleted on the way through, so the question *"what was this building relying on, and when?"* stays
answerable afterwards. §3 sets out the rule this follows.

**The windows are a fire safety measure while they are the provision**, and the acceptance depends
on their still opening. A window painted shut, sealed, obstructed by stored items, or fitted with a
security restrictor in good faith is a failed smoke ventilation provision that looks exactly like a
window — so *Stair smoke ventilation — natural, by openable windows* checks them.

⚠ **What the residential corridors and lobbies are provided with is not recorded, and we are
asking.** Everything above describes the two *stairs*. A mechanical smoke ventilation system serving
the corridors — a shaft, per-floor dampers, extract fans, a control panel, a cause-and-effect and a
standby supply — is a separate installation from stair ventilation, would not be covered by any
stair row, and is the system a firefighting operation in a lobby depends on. §6 now carries a row
for it, held as *not established either way* rather than as absent. **If the corridors have no
mechanical system, that is itself a fire strategy statement and we would like to record it as the
reason** — a silence in a register reads as "nothing here" when it may only mean that nobody asked.

> ⚠ **Three stair cadences have no justification written in this document, and we have left them
> alone anyway — we would welcome a view.** The daily stair-core walk, and the two weekly stair
> checks, were written from a supplied document describing a **single** protected route with **no**
> smoke ventilation. That description was wrong: there are two stairs and both are ventilated. So
> the reason for the cadence has gone entirely rather than merely weakened. **We have not relaxed
> it**, because relaxing a safety cadence belongs to whoever owns the fire risk assessment and not
> to a documentation pass. But a control nobody can state a reason for is a cost as well as a
> comfort, and the honest question is no longer *"is daily proportionate?"* but *"what is the reason
> for daily?"* Either answer is fine; we would like it recorded.

## 3. What this register is, and what it is not

**It is a catalogue of what CAN apply to a building of this kind — not a snapshot of what applies
today.** Which rows are live for this building at this moment is held separately, as a decision
record, and that separation is deliberate.

So you will find rows here for provisions the building does not currently have. That is not an
oversight. Three rules govern what may be written:

| | |
|---|---|
| **Deleted** | Only where the condition can **never** become true here, or the duty can **never** be ours — a provision the building will never have, or one that binds another party entirely. Two entries have ever been deleted on those grounds. The bar stays that high, and an absence is not argued row by row: **§1 states the scope**, and that answers every such question at once |
| **Present but switched off** | Where the condition is simply **not true today**. The row states its condition and stays in the register; a recorded, reasoned, attributed decision — carrying a review date — marks it *not applicable*, and the same mechanism switches it back on. Nothing is re-drafted in either direction |
| **Written generally** | A row never names a **current defect**. *"Temporary structural support — position and integrity check"* belongs here; "the props to the basement mezzanine" does not, because repairing them would leave the row describing nothing. Which prop, which column, which survey lives in the scheduled work and its records |

**The test applied to every word of every row:** *would this still be true if the building were
repaired, or altered, tomorrow?* If not, it belongs in the records rather than in this list.

**A reviewer asked, fairly, what stops a catalogue from becoming large while the operational
position stays vague.** The answer is that there are three separate objects, and this document is
only the first of them:

| | |
|---|---|
| **1. This catalogue** | What *can* apply to a building of this kind. Every row, whether live here or not. It is a library, and it is what you are reading |
| **2. The applicability decision** | For each row: applies, does not apply, or unresolved — **for this building**. Held as an append-only decision record carrying the decision, who made it, the date, the reason, a review date, and the condition that would switch the row back on. That last one lives on the row itself, as *Applies when* |
| **3. The live obligation** | The actual scheduled instance: owner, due date, evidence, status, escalation route. Created from a row **only once** the decision in (2) says it applies |

⛔ **The catalogue may omit these fields. The operational system may not.** Every live obligation
created from a row here must carry, as a minimum:

| Field | Minimum content |
|---|---|
| **Evidence holder** | Named system, organisation and record location |
| **Assurance owner** | Named person or role, and the frequency or trigger of their assurance |
| **Escalation recipient** | Named decision-maker, and the escalation route |
| **Failure status** | Open · overdue · failed · inaccessible · not applicable |
| **Interim control** | Required wherever the underlying control is not operating |
| **Close-out verifier** | Someone independent enough of the work to verify completion |

The catalogue can state who bears each duty in law because that follows from the instrument. It
cannot state any of the six above, because each is a fact about how this building is run — which is
why they are a requirement on the live obligation rather than a gap in this document.

⚠ **The consequence worth stating: a row in this document is not a live obligation, and its presence
here is not a claim that anything is scheduled.** A reader auditing the building should ask for (2)
and (3), not for this. What this document is for is making sure nothing is missing from (1) — because
a duty absent from the library cannot be decided on in (2) or scheduled in (3), and its absence is
invisible at every later stage.

Two consequences a reviewer should expect, because both look like mistakes and are not:

- **A row marked not applicable is still a row**, and is still shown. That is how the register
  shows a requirement was **considered and decided**, rather than never thought of — and those two
  are indistinguishable once a row is left out. A register holding only what applies today also
  cannot tell you what you stopped doing, or what you would have to start doing if the building
  changed, which is most of the value of keeping one.
- **Some rows are here because the building is expected to change.** Electric vehicle charging is
  the clearest: there are no charge points today and there will be. Writing the row now means
  installing them is a recorded switch-on and a scheduled inspection, not a discovery months later
  that nothing was checking them.
- **A pair of rows may describe alternatives**, only one of which can be live. Mechanical and
  natural smoke ventilation of a stair is the example running through §2 and §6.

## 4. How to read a row

Three separate questions are kept apart on purpose, because collapsing them is how a register comes
to claim more legal force than it has.

**"Why we hold it" — where the duty comes from:**

| | |
|---|---|
| **Legislation** (53) | An Act or statutory instrument imposes this. Not doing it is unlawful |
| **Standard or code** (25) | A British Standard or approved code sets the method and usually the interval. The standard is not itself the law — **but read the reference before concluding nothing legal is at stake.** For a fire safety measure, **article 17 of the Fire Safety Order** requires it to be kept in an efficient state, in efficient working order and in good repair, and those rows now name that duty. The standard says *how often and how*; the Order says *that it must work* |
| **Contract or scheme** (5) | An insurer, certification scheme or maintenance contract requires it. Binding by agreement, not by law |
| **Our own decision** (35) | Nothing external requires this. We chose it, and it is ours to change |

**"Where the interval comes from" — whether the frequency is law or convention.** This is the
distinction this document exists to hold. A fire risk assessment must be
reviewed *"regularly"*; annual is convention. The Electricity at Work Regulations require an
installation to be maintained so as to prevent danger and set **no interval at all** — the five
years comes from BS 7671. There is **no legal requirement to test portable appliances**, nor to do so
annually. A row saying *established practice* is telling you the frequency is our judgement.

**"Maximum interval" — and a correction this revision makes to its own worst habit.** No instrument
anywhere says 92 days, or 366, or 183. Those are **our arithmetic on a calendar word**, and they
were printed under the heading *Maximum permitted interval*, which told the reader that the
legislation contained a 92-day ceiling. It does not. **A document written to stop a convention being
read as law was dressing its own arithmetic as law** — the same error it exists to catch, pointed
inwards.

So a statutory period is now given **in the instrument's own words**, verbatim:

| | |
|---|---|
| **Statutory interval** (10 rows) | The period as the instrument states it — *"at least every 3 months"*, *"within each period of 12 months"*, *"at least every 6 months"*, *"monthly routine checks"*. Quoted, not converted |
| **Internal scheduling ceiling** (8 rows) | The day count we schedule to. **Ours, and deliberately shorter than the statutory period.** See below |
| **Our scheduling tolerance** (12 rows) | The same thing where the source states **no** permitted maximum at all, so there is nothing to overshoot — the two monthly FSER reg 7 rows, and the ten rows whose period a British Standard expresses in words. **The day figure on those is our arithmetic, not a number the source contains** |
| **Interval specified by the reference** (10 rows) | The period a British Standard states, in the standard's own words rather than converted to days — *"annual"*, *"six-monthly"*, *"weekly"*. A standard is not the law, so this is not a statutory interval; the day count we schedule to is shown separately as ours |
| **Our adopted maximum** (0 rows) | A ceiling that is purely ours. Defined so a future row cannot borrow one of the other labels by default |

⚠ **No row now carries "Maximum permitted interval" at all**, and that is the point: on inspection,
**not one** of the periods this building relies on is expressed by its instrument as a number of
days. FSER reg 10 says *"at least every 3 months"*; LOLER says *"at least every 6 months"*; FSER
reg 7 says *"monthly routine checks"* and expresses no permitted maximum whatever. Every day figure
in this register is ours.

⛔ **The scheduling rule is a calendar-period calculation, and the day count is a floor under it,
not a substitute for it.** A due date is the statutory period measured from the last completion —
*three months after 1 February is 1 May* — because that is what the instrument says. A day count
cannot reproduce that: real three-month spans run from **89 to 92 days** depending where they
start, six-month spans from **181 to 184**, and twelve-month spans are **365 or 366**.

⚠ **This revision found that our own figures could breach the deadline they were meant to protect.**
92 days added to 1 February gives 4 May — three days past the statutory 1 May. Six months at 183 and
twelve months at 366 had the same fault, by two days and one. **Every ceiling is now the shortest
span that period can have in a real calendar** — 3 months → 89, 6 months → 181, 12 months → 365,
2 years → 730 — so no starting date can produce an overshoot. Scheduling a few days early costs
nothing; being one day late is a breach, and this register must not be the thing that causes one.

⚠ **The day figure is internal and is not the deadline.** It is shown because a scheduler can only
count days, and it is qualified on every row for one specific reason: **a number in a table gets
copied into procedures and contracts.** The authoritative field is the statutory period in the
instrument's words directly above it, and the due date is that period calculated as a calendar
period — three months after 1 February is 1 May, not 1 February plus a day count.

⚠ **Tightening the ceilings exposed something the old figures had hidden: two rows were PLANNED at
the very edge of the statutory period** — communal fire doors at 90 days against "at least every
3 months", and the lift examination at 182 against "at least every 6 months". That leaves no room
at all, and turns an appointment slipping by a day into a breach. Both planned cadences now sit
inside their ceiling, at **12 weeks** and **25 weeks** — still quarterly and six-monthly in any
ordinary sense, with days of slack rather than none. A test enforces that no row plans looser than
its own ceiling.

A row with an interval and no ceiling at all is one where nothing sets a limit. The communal EICR
and the water-temperature monitoring are both of that kind: an interval we have chosen, and no
instrument anywhere setting an outer bound on it.

**"Statutory dutyholder" and "Operationally responsible" are different questions, and on 38 of these
rows they are different people** — a figure computed from the register and checked by
`npm run check:obligations`, not an estimate. The first is who the instrument binds: the
responsible person under the Fire Safety Order (45 rows), the accountable person or principal
accountable person under the Building Safety Act (17), the employer or person in control under
health and safety law (11), the asbestos duty holder (2). The second is who does the work — often a
contractor or site staff. **Engaging a contractor discharges the task, never the duty.** Where a row
says *"None — this is our own control"*, nothing external requires it at all.

**"Retention basis" says why a record is kept for as long as it is**, because a bare "10 years"
reads as a statutory minimum. ⚠ **It is worth stating plainly: no instrument cited anywhere in this
register sets a retention period.** Every figure here is internal policy, except where a record forms
part of the current golden thread — in which case it is kept while it remains current under
SI 2023/907 reg 7, and the year figure is a floor rather than the rule — or where an insurer or
contract requires longer.

**"Trigger source" is the half of an event row that was missing.** A row can name exactly what fires
a duty and still rest on somebody remembering to notice it. Each event row now names the system or
process that must generate the event. ⛔ **On 9 of the 20, the honest answer is that nothing does** —
including the building-work change-control screen, which four other rows name as *their* source. That
is stated rather than smoothed over, because it is the finding.

**"Competence required" is absent on 35 rows, and that is a gap rather than a statement.** Where it
is present it is load-bearing — *"a competent person INDEPENDENT of the maintenance contractor"* is
the difference between a thorough examination and a service visit. Where it is absent, it mostly
means the row is a governance or management control performed by the duty holder, for whom no
external competence standard exists; but that is not true of all 35, and we would rather say so than
imply the blanks were considered. **Treat a missing competence line as unanswered, not as "anyone".**

**Retention is not one period.** Six kinds of record age differently and should not share a single
number: the **current** safety-critical record · **superseded versions** of it · **audit evidence**
that something was done · **personal data**, which has its own minimisation duty pulling the other
way · **legal or investigation material** under hold · and records kept because a **contract or
insurer** requires them. Collapsing six different ageing rules into one number is a simplification
this register has not yet undone.

**Meanwhile, "Retention" is our policy unless a row says otherwise.** No retention period in this register
should be read as a statutory minimum. They are internal policy choices made to be compatible with
data protection obligations, and a few follow an insurer's or a scheme's requirement. ⚠ Two
qualifications matter more than the numbers: where information forms part of the current golden
thread it is kept while it remains current, not for a fixed term; and where a risk, claim,
investigation or legal hold is open, retention runs until that closes. **A per-row retention basis —
statutory, regulator guidance, contract, or internal policy — would be better than this blanket
statement, and is not yet on the rows.**

**"What makes it fall due" — the mechanism.** One "Interval" column was hiding four different things:

| | | |
|---|---|---|
| **Calendar** | 92 | A clock. Weekly, monthly, five-yearly |
| **Event** | 22 | Something happens — works begin, the walls change, a report is revised |
| **Risk or condition** | 3 | A judgement: condition, deterioration, or a previous finding says look again |
| **On direction** | 1 | Someone external tells us to, and until they do there is nothing to schedule |

⛔ **Five calendar rows are marked *assurance control only*, and the label is load-bearing.** Where a
statutory duty fires on an event, this register holds two rows: the event row, which is the duty,
and an annual pass, which is our control over it. The split is right, but a reader skimming
*Calendar / Annual* on the second one could conclude that the statutory process is checked annually.
It is not. Those rows now say **"Calendar — assurance control only"**, **"Annual assurance
confirmation"**, and carry a line naming the operative control: **⚠ Does NOT discharge**. The duty
fires when a resident is identified or asks for an assessment, when the layout changes, when
somebody becomes aware of a change to the key building information — never on a date.

⚠ **92 of 118 are calendar-driven.** That is an honest picture of how this register was
built — around recurring activities — and it is exactly why the second principle below now sits
above every row. The point holds from the other end too: the duties most likely to be missed are the
ones that fire on an event or run through an interface, not the ones with a date — and several rows
here exist because that gap was found rather than designed for.

## 5. Two principles that govern the whole list

Both came from review. They matter more than any individual row.

### An inspection is not finished when the check is recorded

Where a check identifies a defect, deterioration, non-compliance or emerging risk, the resulting
**decision, action, timescale, interim control, completion and verification must also be recorded**.
Where the finding affects building safety risk or prescribed building information, the relevant
statutory records must be updated too.

A register that records only that an inspection happened proves attendance, not safety.

### Calendar cycles do not replace event-driven review

**A significant change, defect, incident, new information or other circumstance affecting building
safety can require an earlier assessment or action even where the next scheduled check is not due.**

This is the statutory position, not a counsel of perfection. The duty to assess building safety risks
requires further assessment *"at regular intervals"*, at any time there is reason to suspect the
current assessment is no longer valid, **and** at the direction of the regulator. Two of those three
are not calendar events, and no schedule will raise them — which is why there is now a standing
entry for them in §6, sitting above the individual inspection rows.

**This is the defect successive reviews have found most often in this register — five times now**,
each in a different place: the fire and rescue service floor plans, the 2025 evacuation process, the
occurrence-reporting and complaints workflows our annual rows were auditing, the fire risk
assessment's own statutory triggers, and now the key building information notification, whose
28-day clock runs from the day somebody becomes *aware* of a change. In every case an annual pass
stood where the law puts an event, and in every case the annual pass looked perfectly healthy. **A
register assembled from periodic activities is structurally blind to this**, so the question worth
asking of every calendar row in §6 is not "is the interval right?" but *"where is the trigger, and
what fires it?"*

### An interim measure needs an end condition, not just a frequency

**This applies to all nine rows in §6 *Conditional and interim measures*, and it is stated here once
rather than repeated onto each of them** — a warning copied nine times is read no times, and it says
the same words in the same order whether or not anything has moved since.

A frequent check standing beside an open defect is not assurance. It can be the opposite: the
checking is visible, the defect is not, and the register looks controlled precisely while nothing is
being resolved. **Without an escalation threshold and an end condition, a measure of this kind can
run for years.**

Each of the nine rows is marked **operationally incomplete** and carries a **Completion action**
line that reads **NOT ASSIGNED**. That is deliberate and it is the thing to look at: prose reads the
same two years later, whereas an unassigned action is conspicuous every time this document is
produced. **Seven things are missing from every one of them:**

1. the underlying hazard, and the finding it comes from
2. the risk it reduces, and the residual risk being accepted
3. the named technical authority
4. the threshold that forces escalation rather than another observation
5. who may declare the situation unsafe and stop occupation or use
6. the permanent solution and its target date
7. whether the measure is a condition of the safety case or of the fire risk assessment — and
   whether residents, contractors and the fire and rescue authority have been told

None of the seven can be answered from this document. They need the engineer's specification and a
decision-maker, which is why they are asked for rather than filled in.

## 7. What this list does not tell you

Stated plainly, because a statement of intent read as a statement of performance would be the most
damaging way for this document to be wrong.

- **This is what we intend to do, not a record of what has been done.**
- **The intervals have not been independently verified.** The references were checked against the
  legislation; the frequencies were not re-checked one by one, and rows marked *established practice*
  are our judgement.
- **Whether each obligation applies to this building** is our assessment.
- **Almost nothing is ever removed from this register.** Where a duty exists but does not bite here,
  it stays on the list and a separate reasoned decision is recorded against it, with a date and a
  name — a deleted requirement is indistinguishable from one nobody thought of. The bar for removing
  one outright is that it can never apply here, or can never be ours; two have met it.
- **There is no periodic external wall fire appraisal here, and that is deliberate.** A PAS 9980
  appraisal is commissioned to answer a question, not run on a cycle, and no instrument imposes a
  repeat. What brings it back is a *change* — caught by the external wall record row and by the fire
  risk assessment's statutory trigger, which since the Fire Safety Act 2021 expressly reaches the
  structure, the external walls and the flat entrance doors. Adding a periodic row would invent a
  duty and imply the trigger rows were not enough. Two things *do* carry forward from an appraisal:
  any interim measure it recommends belongs in *Conditional and interim measures* with an end
  condition, and the risk level and mitigating steps it produces are what the external wall record
  is legally required to contain.
- **Resident personal data is deliberately held elsewhere** — names, contacts, and the content of
  personal evacuation plans. That is a decision about where records live, not a decision to ignore a
  duty: the 2025 evacuation duties are all in §6, and the four that touch residents are marked as
  interfaces — we hold that the process exists, is current, who owns it and when it was last
  confirmed, and nothing else.

## 8. What is outstanding

**This section used to be called *Open questions*, and a reviewer was right that the name had
stopped being honest.** Several of these are not questions anyone is still asking — they are
decisions that need an owner, or duties that are not currently being discharged. A question can sit
in a document indefinitely without anyone noticing; an unassigned action with a blank owner cannot,
because the blank is conspicuous every time the document is produced.

So they are set out below as **the actions schedule**, which is what §6 means when a row points at
one. **Owner, technical authority and due date are deliberately empty** —
those are facts about this building's arrangements, and filling them with plausible names would
defeat the purpose. **Consequence if unanswered** is filled in, because that is the part we can
assess and the part that decides the order.

⚠ **This schedule is the reviewer-facing half.** The wider set of work — the records to create, the
software that does not exist, and the interfaces with other parties — is held separately and is not
reproduced here, because a reviewer does not need it and two copies of one action list is how the two
fall out of step. Every action below keeps its number there.
